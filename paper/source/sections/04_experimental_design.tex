\section{Experimental design}
\label{sec:design}
Each of the three comparisons in Section~\ref{sec:intro} draws on a different kind of evidence, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:study_design}. The controlled benchmark isolates the effect of the learning objective and compares model specifications against Teacher targets. The stated-choice experiments assess agreement with people on data that play no part in fitting or model selection, and the simulation experiment follows predicted responses through assignment and routing.

\subsection{Controlled Teacher-response benchmark}
\label{sec:controlled_design}
A persona is a generated traveler profile, and a state combines a persona with a trip, a context, alternative attributes and Teacher targets. The training, validation and test sets contain 1,839, 365 and 437 states drawn from disjoint groups of 28, six and six personas. The test states form 398 response pairs and 94 interaction contrasts. Single-context changes, PT-supply profiles, joint perturbations and context--attribute contrasts all follow the same persona partition (Supplementary Table~\ref{supp-tab:training_sources}). The prepared manifest also excludes the fare--congestion joint combination from training and validation (112 and 24 candidate states, respectively). This provides a limited compositional holdout within the joint source; the reported aggregate comparison does not establish generalization across all unseen intervention families or intensity ranges.

Within each of three training seeds, the four neural objectives share the architecture, inputs, availability rules, training pairs, initialization and optimization budget, so that the objective is the only difference between them. Models for the probability-response comparison are selected by macro-source KL, a validation KL divergence summarized at the level of the four supervision sources. MNL-S receives the same endpoint information and exposure weights, is regularized separately and is selected by the same criterion. Optimization settings are reported in Supplementary Section~\ref{supp-sec:supp_controlled}.

Departure prediction is evaluated in a separate comparison selected by validation departure MAE. This criterion chooses the bounded-linear and neural timing predictors, the ridge penalty, and independently trained versions of the four joint objectives. The neural and bounded predictors share data, information and update budget, which separates the effect of the timing specification from that of the selection rule.

Response fidelity is measured as the absolute difference between Teacher and Student probability changes, averaged first over the modes available in a pair and then over pairs. Macro-source KL, departure MAE against the Teacher and interaction error complement this measure. Interaction error compares the response to a combined perturbation with the responses to its components. Formal definitions, including the feasibility violation rate, are given in Supplementary Section~\ref{supp-sec:metric_definitions}.

Two sources of uncertainty are reported. Training-seed standard deviations describe optimization variability, whereas paired intervals clustered by persona describe variation across the six held-out personas and are conditional on them. Consistency within the benchmark is further examined through persona-level results, paired seed differences, removal of each test persona from evaluation without retraining, and reaggregation of the repeated Teacher outputs that are available.

\subsection{Stated-choice experiments}
\label{sec:human_design}
The Singapore sample comprises 332 eligible residents who made 3,320 choices. Its ten tasks form five within-person pairs on PT accessibility, rain, fares, PT delay and roadworks. The Shanghai cohort evaluated by the models comprises 321 respondents with 3,208 explicit four-mode choices over ten tasks. Two further answers indicate no suitable mode and are not scored. Twelve eligible respondents whose answers fall outside the model's input mapping are retained in a human-only sensitivity analysis. Responses were collected from 20 August to 5 September 2026 in Singapore and from 12 to 18 September 2026 in Shanghai, and both questionnaires recorded participant consent. We distributed electronic questionnaires through WeChat, WhatsApp and related personal contacts to research-group members' families, friends, classmates and colleagues, and through onward sharing within those networks. Singapore used Google Forms and Shanghai used Wenjuanxing. The target of inference is response agreement within these convenience and snowball samples, not citywide mode shares. Singapore eligibility required residence in Singapore; Shanghai eligibility required travel in Shanghai during the preceding 30 days. Participants completed ten stated-choice tasks online. Sample flow and questionnaire access are reported in Supplementary Section~\ref{supp-sec:supp_inputs}.

\paragraph{Ethics and consent statement.} The stated-choice questionnaires were administered as anonymous, minimal-risk surveys. No direct personal identifiers were collected. Before beginning the questionnaire, all participants were shown an electronic information and consent statement describing the study purpose, voluntary participation, intended research use of the responses, and confidentiality protections; only respondents who provided consent proceeded to the survey. Formal institutional ethics approval was not obtained. The study procedures were designed to protect participant privacy and to limit data collection to information necessary for the stated research purpose.

The two surveys specify their tasks in different ways. Singapore shows respondents numerical tables of time and cost. Shanghai describes most conditions qualitatively, with explicit values only for a 6-yuan PT fare, a 30-yuan parking fee and one transfer. Model inputs therefore combine attributes displayed in the questionnaire, characteristics reported by respondents and reference values for information that was not collected (Supplementary Table~\ref{supp-tab:input_provenance}). The reference values do not depend on respondents' answers. Twelve alternative specifications for Singapore and eight for Shanghai vary selected missing attributes and reference times (Supplementary Table~\ref{supp-tab:ownership_defaults}).

The questionnaire's car option is represented by the model's private-car alternative. Private car requires ownership and a licence, and bicycle requires access. All explicit choices are scored, including eight Singapore and two Shanghai choices of a mode that the model treats as unavailable, so that the evaluation sample is not reduced to choices the model can represent.

For respondent $i$, contrast $c$, task weights $w_{ct}$ and target mode group $M_c$, the stated and predicted responses and the response bias are
\begin{align}
r^H_{ic}&=\sum_t w_{ct}\mathbf1(y_{it}\in M_c),\label{eq:human_response}\\
r^j_{ic}&=\sum_t w_{ct}\sum_{m\in M_c}p^j_{itm},\qquad j\in\{T,S\},\label{eq:model_response}\\
B^j_c&=\operatorname{mean}_i(r^j_{ic}-r^H_{ic}).\label{eq:human_bias}
\end{align}
Simple pairs use weights $-1$ and $+1$, and the rain--delay interaction combines four tasks. Intervals come from a respondent bootstrap that resamples each person's full set of tasks, with shared draws for paired model comparisons. Nominal 95\% intervals are reported together with Bonferroni-adjusted intervals over the five Singapore or eight Shanghai contrasts, computed separately for each model and city. These intervals are conditional on the fitted models, and an interval that includes zero does not establish equivalence. Choice accuracy, mode shares, Brier score and negative log-likelihood (NLL) complement the response measures. Because a mode with zero predicted probability makes NLL infinite, NLL is computed with explicit probability truncation (Supplementary Section~\ref{supp-sec:supp_human}).

A further Shanghai analysis asks whether inputs derived from a transport network improve agreement because they are matched to individual respondents. Origin--destination pairs of approximately 6~km are assigned using OSM roads and a synthetic metro timetable, and the assignment is then repeated and complete supply profiles are exchanged between respondents. The constructed trips test sensitivity to numerical inputs and are not observations of respondents' journeys.

\subsection{Contemporary API reference on the survey tasks}
\label{sec:teacher_design}
A contemporary API model is queried on the stated-choice tasks for 24 respondents per city, stratified by age and car ownership. This provides a same-input comparison with the frozen Students; it is distinct from the archived Teacher targets used for training. Each of the ten tasks per respondent receives three successful Teacher elicitations. The Teacher receives the same numerical and categorical attributes and the same availability rule as the Students, but not the human answers. Metadata that the Students do not use are set to neutral values, and the prompt explains PT feasibility and its time components. Service settings and repeat variability are reported in Supplementary Section~\ref{supp-sec:teacher_revision}.

For this subsection, $T$ denotes the contemporary API reference, $T_{\mathrm{now}}$, rather than a verified reconstruction of the training backend. On the same respondents, the signed response discrepancy decomposes as
\begin{equation}
\bar r^S_c-\bar r^H_c=(\bar r^T_c-\bar r^H_c)+(\bar r^S_c-\bar r^T_c).
\label{eq:attribution}
\end{equation}
The first term compares the current API reference with stated behavior; the second compares the Student with that reference. This is an exact descriptive identity, not an identification of compression error. Both campaigns used the official DeepSeek V4 Pro service, but without a common immutable backend record the second term can also include differences between acquisition periods and elicitation protocols \citep{bisbee2024synthetic}. All terms use common respondent resamples, with elicitation variability reported separately.

\subsection{Simulation and computational experiments}
\label{sec:execution_design}
The Helsinki experiment compares a baseline and a perceived-delay condition for a fixed population of 1,000 travelers, using OSM roads and HSL timetables. The delay condition adds 15 minutes to the PT delay perceived by the behavioral model while leaving the physical timetable unchanged. SA-Student, the soft-KL and direction+magnitude neural Students, and MNL-S with independent neural timing are each combined with the three assignment rules of Section~\ref{sec:execution_method}. Common random numbers are used across paired conditions and assignment rules, and training and assignment variability are summarized separately (Supplementary Table~\ref{supp-tab:execution_design}).

At each stage, the response $R$ is the change in PT probability or PT-use share from baseline to delay over the same 1,000 people. A single MATSim iteration with fixed behavioral predictions isolates the gap $G$ introduced by assignment, routing and execution. Holding the timetable fixed is the control that separates execution effects from changes in physical supply. The estimand is response transmission through this pipeline, not adaptation to a physical service disruption or equilibrium demand. Additional comparisons route trips at desired and at adjusted departure times. Walking and cycling use network-derived link speeds, and a Shanghai experiment examines alternative active-mode speed assumptions while keeping people, assigned modes, departure times and plans fixed.

A Singapore experiment records the time needed to construct simulation scenarios for nested populations of up to 50,000 agents, separating behavioral inference from feature and route preparation and measuring construction with and without reuse of unchanged supply calculations. Teacher latency is measured on successful requests only, and population-scale Teacher times are projections rather than measured builds (Supplementary Sections~\ref{supp-sec:supp_execution} and~\ref{supp-sec:supp_compute}).
