\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Transport scenario analysis asks how travel demand changes when conditions change. Matching a baseline mode share does not establish whether a model will reproduce the response to a delay, a fare increase or a longer walk to a stop. In agent-based simulation, such responses pass through assignment, routing and interaction with transport supply before becoming simulated demand \citep{horni2016multi,axhausen2002some}. The relevant behavioral target is therefore a change between conditions, alongside the level of demand in each condition.

Large language models (LLMs) offer a flexible way to specify traveler behavior. Prompts can combine personal circumstances, trip attributes and contextual changes, supporting applications in travel prediction, route choice and agent-based modeling \citep{mo2026llmtravel,wang2025agentic,liu2025framework,nie2025roles}. Behavioral distillation moves repeated inference offline: an LLM Teacher supplies numerical judgments, and a compact Student uses these targets to make local predictions. This is a concrete instance of the hybrid LLM--conventional-model designs proposed for transport simulation \citep{liu2025framework}. It reduces reliance on live LLM calls during deployment, but introduces a distinct validation problem: which properties of the Teacher survive compression, and which of those properties are useful for transport analysis?

The distinction matters because predictive agreement is not behavioral agreement. In travel choice modeling, models with strong predictive performance can differ in elasticities and other behavioral indicators \citep{wang2020deep,martinbaos2023prediction}. LLM-based travel studies likewise distinguish plausible responses from empirically aligned behavior \citep{valuingtime2026,liu2026persona,xu2026satisfaction}. Distillation introduces another layer: even a Student that resembles its Teacher at individual states may differ in how its predictions change between states. Conversely, a less faithful Student may accidentally agree more closely with human responses. Neither model compression nor improved prediction alone resolves this issue.

We distinguish \emph{static fidelity}, agreement with the Teacher at individual states, from \emph{response fidelity}, agreement in finite changes of elicited choice distributions for the same traveler and trip. These contrasts are defined for specified perturbations; they are not, by themselves, population causal effects or elasticities. Figure~\ref{fig:concept} illustrates the traveler--trip pairing and its connection to aggregate demand. Exact agreement at every endpoint would imply exact response agreement. In finite data and finite-capacity models, however, average endpoint scores do not directly evaluate the contrasts that a scenario analysis queries.

Response fidelity is a criterion for faithful compression, not a guarantee of behavioral validity. A Student can inherit a Teacher's discrepancy from people. Independently, a predicted change can be altered by the rule used to assign modes, by route search and by simulation execution. The central question of this study is therefore whether model performance carries across three evaluation levels: \emph{Teacher-response fidelity, agreement with stated human responses, and preservation through network execution}. These levels are complementary tests, not interchangeable definitions of success.

The study makes three contributions. First, it operationalizes response-centric evaluation with a persona-disjoint benchmark and matched neural objectives, alongside a structured logit Student and separate timing predictors. This design distinguishes the effect of explicit response weighting from that of model specification. Second, independent stated-choice samples in Singapore and Shanghai test whether Teacher-response fidelity orders models in the same way as agreement with people. A later, same-task LLM comparison provides a descriptive reference, separately from the archived training targets. Third, a controlled Helsinki MATSim experiment traces each response from predicted probabilities to PT boarding, separating assignment and execution effects from changes in the behavioral model. These are linked evaluations of model behavior, not a claim of uniform geographic transfer across the three settings.

The strongest finding is a change in what counts as the best model. Response-aware supervision improves Teacher-response error modestly; a linear choice specification improves it more. Yet the Student closest to the Teacher reverses a stated accessibility response, and an assignment rule that improves routability does not necessarily improve response preservation. The contribution is thus a validation procedure and empirical diagnosis for LLM-derived traveler models, with learning objectives and modular Students as testable implementations. It identifies which comparison must be passed before an imitation score can support a transport-simulation claim.
